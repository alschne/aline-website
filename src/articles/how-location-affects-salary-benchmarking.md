# How Location Affects Salary Benchmarking

When companies think about compensation, one of the first questions usually sounds simple:

“What should we pay this role?”

But the answer depends heavily on one factor many organizations underestimate: location.

The same role can carry very different compensation depending on location. A Software Engineer in San Francisco, for example, will typically benchmark much differently than a Software Engineer in Pittsburgh or Boise.

And in today’s hiring market, where remote work, hybrid structures, and multi-state teams are common, location-based salary benchmarking has become significantly more complex.

This article breaks down how geography affects compensation benchmarking, why it matters, and what companies should consider when building compensation strategies that are competitive, fair, and sustainable.

---

## What Is Location-Based Salary Benchmarking?

Location-based salary benchmarking is the process of comparing compensation against market data while accounting for geographic differences.

Instead of asking:

> “What does a Marketing Manager make?”

The better question is:

> “What does a Marketing Manager make in this labor market?”

Because compensation is shaped by regional labor dynamics, including:

- Cost of living
- Local talent supply
- Industry concentration
- Competition for talent
- Remote work expectations
- State and local pay transparency laws

A benchmark that is accurate in one market can be completely misaligned in another.

---

## Why Salaries Vary by Location

### 1. Cost of Living Influences Expectations

This is the factor most people think of first.

Employees in high-cost areas typically expect higher compensation because housing, transportation, taxes, and everyday expenses are higher.

For example:
- Compensation benchmarks in New York City or San Francisco are often materially higher than benchmarks in smaller Midwest markets.
- A role paying $90,000 in one region may need to pay $130,000+ in another to remain competitive.

But cost of living alone does *not* determine compensation.

Companies sometimes over-index on cost-of-living calculators when the real driver is labor market competition.

---

### 2. Talent Competition Matters More Than Many Companies Realize

Some locations have dense concentrations of employers competing for similar talent.

For example:
- Tech hubs often command higher engineering salaries because companies are aggressively competing for experienced technical talent.
- Healthcare-heavy markets may show stronger compensation for clinical, operational, or revenue cycle roles.
- Financial centers often inflate compensation for accounting, analytics, and finance positions.

This means two cities with similar living costs can still produce very different compensation benchmarks.

---

### 3. Remote Work Changed the Equation

Remote hiring fundamentally changed compensation benchmarking.

Before widespread remote work, most companies benchmarked against:
- The company’s headquarters location
- The employee’s office location
- A limited regional labor market

Now companies regularly hire across multiple states — or internationally — creating new compensation questions:

- Should remote employees be paid based on where they live?
- Should everyone in the same role receive the same pay?
- Should pay be adjusted when employees relocate?
- Which labor market should benchmark data reflect?

There is no single universal answer.

Different organizations use different compensation philosophies:
- National pay models
- Geographic pay bands
- Hybrid location tiers
- Cost-of-labor models
- Headquarters-based compensation structures

The important part is consistency and clarity.

## Common Mistakes Companies Make With Geographic Benchmarking

### Using National Averages Without Local Context

National averages can be misleading.

A national benchmark may blend compensation data from:
- High-cost metro areas
- Rural regions
- Remote roles
- Enterprise organizations
- Startups
- Different industry mixes

Without geographic filtering, compensation data often becomes too broad to support accurate pay decisions.

---

### Treating Cost of Living as the Same Thing as Market Pay

Cost of living and market compensation are related — but not identical.

A lower-cost city can still have strong salary expectations if:
- Talent is scarce
- Specialized skills are difficult to hire for
- Major employers are competing aggressively

Likewise, some higher-cost areas may not support premium compensation for certain functions.

Companies that rely only on cost-of-living adjustments often miss actual labor market realities.

---

### Ignoring Remote Market Compression

Remote hiring increased salary transparency and competition.

Candidates now compare offers across:
- Regions
- Industries
- Remote-first companies
- National employers

This can create “market compression,” where historically lower-paying regions begin moving closer to national compensation levels for in-demand roles.

Organizations that continue benchmarking only against their immediate local market may struggle to attract experienced talent.

---

## How Companies Typically Handle Geographic Pay

There are several common approaches.

### National Pay Structure

Some companies pay employees similarly regardless of location.

Advantages:
- Simpler administration
- Easier internal equity conversations
- Stronger recruiting appeal

Challenges:
- Higher payroll costs in lower-cost regions
- Potential overpayment relative to local markets

---

### Geographic Pay Bands

Other organizations create location tiers:
- Tier 1: Major metro/high-cost markets
- Tier 2: Mid-sized markets
- Tier 3: Lower-cost regions

Advantages:
- More alignment with regional markets
- Better payroll cost control

Challenges:
- Employees may perceive differences as unfair
- Requires ongoing maintenance and market analysis

---

### Cost-of-Labor Approach

Some companies benchmark directly against local labor markets rather than cost-of-living data.

This approach focuses on:
- What competitors are paying
- Actual hiring conditions
- Talent availability

This is often the most market-accurate method, especially for hard-to-fill roles.

---

## Why Accurate Location Benchmarking Matters

Poor geographic benchmarking can create several business problems:

### Hiring Delays

If compensation is too low for a market, hiring timelines increase and offer acceptance rates decline.

---

### Internal Equity Issues

Employees compare compensation more than ever before.

Inconsistent geographic pay practices can create:
- Retention issues
- Morale problems
- Pay compression concerns
- Trust issues around compensation decisions

---

### Budgeting Problems

Overpaying relative to market can inflate compensation costs unnecessarily.

Underpaying can increase:
- Turnover
- Recruiting costs
- Lost productivity
- Backfill expenses

Good benchmarking helps companies balance competitiveness with financial sustainability.

---

## What Good Salary Benchmarking Looks Like

Effective compensation benchmarking usually includes:
- Role-specific market data
- Geographic filtering
- Industry context
- Company size considerations
- Leveling accuracy
- Remote work strategy alignment

The goal is not simply to “pay more.”

The goal is to build compensation structures that:
- Attract talent
- Retain employees
- Support internal equity
- Align with business strategy
- Remain financially sustainable

---

## Final Thoughts

Location has always affected compensation — but remote work and national hiring competition have made salary benchmarking more nuanced than ever.

The same job title can carry very different market compensation depending on:
- Geography
- Industry
- Talent demand
- Remote expectations
- Company compensation philosophy

Organizations that understand these dynamics are better positioned to make informed compensation decisions, improve hiring outcomes, and build pay structures employees trust.

And as compensation transparency continues increasing, accurate location-based benchmarking is becoming less of a “nice to have” and more of a business necessity.
